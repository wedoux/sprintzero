# QA Readout

## Finding: "3 of 8 participants preferred the subscription model over in-app purchases when asked directly; the other 5 said they had no preference."

**Evidence strength: STRONG**

The claim is a tightly scoped descriptive statement about what 8 named participants said when asked a specific question. It does not generalise, does not infer causation, and does not extend to a wider population. On the evidence provided, the arithmetic and the characterisation are both accurate.

### Provenance

| Claim | Source | Observed or inferred | Traceable? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 of 8 preferred subscription | INT-01, INT-02, INT-03 (direct quotes expressing preference) | Observed | Yes |
| The other 5 said they had no preference | INT-04 through INT-08 (stated no preference when asked) | Observed | Yes |
| Participants were asked directly | Implicit in INT-04–08 ("explicitly asked to compare..."); consistent with INT-01–03 phrasing | Observed | Yes |

Every component of the claim traces to a specific evidence unit. No unsupported sub-claims.

### Falsification pass

- **Strongest counter-hypothesis:** The three "preference" statements are not actually comparable to the five "no preference" statements, because INT-01–03 may have volunteered the preference unprompted while INT-04–08 were asked a forced-choice question. The evidence note says INT-04–08 were "explicitly asked to compare," but doesn't confirm the same framing was used with INT-01–03. If the elicitation differed, the "3 of 8" count mixes two question types. However, the claim as written only states that participants "were asked directly" and reports what they said — it doesn't rest on the elicitation being identical.
- **What would overturn this finding:** Evidence that one or more of INT-04–08 did express a preference elsewhere in the session that wasn't captured, or that INT-01–03 were leading-questioned into their stated preference. Neither is present in the evidence set.
- **Where the evidence underdetermines the claim:** The claim is descriptive and well-matched to the evidence. The evidence does *not* support any claim beyond this one — e.g., about *why* participants preferred subscription, or about what the preference means for product decisions. The claim wisely makes neither extension.

### Pitfalls checked

| Pitfall | Triggered? | What triggered it |
|---|---|---|
| P-01 Headline Generality | No | Claim names a specific behaviour (stated preference), a specific trigger (direct question), and specific counts. It is not a label. |
| P-02 Context Blindness | No | No prior research is declared in the study record; the claim does not assert novelty. |
| P-03 Transcript Literalism | No | The claim reports what participants said, which is exactly what transcripts capture. It doesn't infer attitudes or intent beyond the stated words. |
| P-04 Volume Mistaken for Strength | No | The claim doesn't inflate confidence — it reports a count (3 of 8), not a verdict like "users prefer subscription." Single-source (interviews) is appropriate because the claim is about what interviewees said, not about user behaviour generally. |
| P-05 Counter-Evidence Filed, Not Faced | No | The 5 "no preference" responses are counter-signal to a "users prefer subscription" conclusion, and the claim correctly reports them in-line rather than burying them. The claim is shaped around the split, not against it. |
| P-06 Formulaic Transfer Hedge | No | No transfer claim is made (no extension to other markets, segments, or populations). |
| P-07 Trust Without Process | No | The reasoning is self-evident from the evidence — the reader can reconstruct the 3/5 split directly from the quoted material. |
| P-08 Narrowing Sold as Resolving | No | No action claim is made. The finding doesn't say "the team can now choose subscription" — it just reports what was heard. |

The finding clears all eight pitfalls because it has been deliberately scoped to describe only what the evidence directly shows. This is a cleaner-than-usual case: the claim is small, the evidence is quoted, and no inferential leap has been attempted.

Worth noting for the research team: the *usefulness* of this finding to a product decision is a separate question. A 3/5 split with a small n=8 is probably not actionable for a monetisation decision on its own, and the finding does not claim otherwise. If a stakeholder reads this and concludes "users prefer subscription," that would be P-01 and P-04 happening downstream of the synthesis — not in it.

### What would change this verdict

Evidence that the elicitation method differed meaningfully between INT-01–03 and INT-04–08 (e.g., unprompted vs. forced-choice) would make the "3 of 8" count misleading and require rephrasing. Nothing in the current evidence set suggests this, but it's the one check worth doing.

### Tacit-knowledge prompt (if a researcher who ran the sessions is available)

Were INT-01–03's preference statements volunteered during open discussion, or elicited by the same direct comparison question used with INT-04–08? If the framing differed, the 3/5 split may be comparing two different question types rather than two different responses to the same question.
