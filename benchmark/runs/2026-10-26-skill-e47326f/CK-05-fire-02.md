## Finding: "This new icon will increase engagement."

**Evidence strength: WEAK**
The claim asserts a behavioural outcome (engagement) that the single piece of evidence does not touch — one participant said the icon was easier to spot, which is a visibility observation, not an engagement outcome.

### Provenance

| Claim | Source | Observed or inferred | Traceable? |
|---|---|---|---|
| The new icon is clearer | INT-01: "that's a much clearer icon" | Observed | Yes |
| The new icon is easier to spot | INT-01: "I spotted it right away" | Observed | Yes |
| The new icon will increase engagement | *not cited* | Inferred (unsupported) | **No** — no evidence links clarity or spotting speed to any engagement metric |

The headline claim has no direct evidence behind it at all. The quote supports noticeability; "engagement" is a different construct that would need its own signal (taps, session length, return visits, task completion — none of which appear here).

### Falsification pass
- **Strongest counter-hypothesis:** Users notice the icon faster but engage with it at the same rate or less — noticeability and engagement are not the same thing. A clearer icon could even reduce engagement if it makes a feature more obviously skippable, or if the "clarity" is just novelty wearing off on re-exposure.
- **What would overturn this finding:** Behavioural data from an A/B test (tap-through rate, feature usage, session depth) showing no lift or a negative lift from the new icon, despite comparable visibility scores. Or qualitative data from users who spotted the icon but chose not to engage.
- **Where the evidence underdetermines the claim:** Entirely. n=1, single source type (interview), and the one quote doesn't address engagement at all. The evidence base can neither support nor refute the claim as stated — it's answering a different question.

### Pitfalls checked

| Pitfall | Triggered? | What triggered it |
|---|---|---|
| P-01 Headline Generality | **Yes** | "Will increase engagement" has no observable behaviour, no trigger, no consequence tied to a specific action. "Engagement" is exactly the kind of label that reads the same in any product report. A specific version would name the behaviour (e.g. "tap-through on the feature behind the icon"). |
| P-02 Context Blindness | No (not assessable) | No prior research declared in the study record. Flagging that if any prior icon or engagement studies exist, they should be checked — the synthesis gives no indication either way. |
| P-03 Transcript Literalism | **Yes** | INT-01 said "I spotted it right away" — a researcher in the room might know whether this was a genuine reaction or a politeness response to an obviously-redesigned icon. The quote is treated as settled evidence with no acknowledgment that stated reactions to design changes are notoriously unreliable. |
| P-04 Volume Mistaken for Strength | **Yes** | n=1, single source type (one interview). Even one more similar quote would still be single-source. The verdict would need independent corroboration (behavioural data, a second method) before any confidence is warranted. |
| P-05 Counter-Evidence Filed, Not Faced | No | No counter-evidence was presented to face or file. (Though note: the *absence* of any counter-evidence in a one-quote dataset is itself a reason for caution, not confidence.) |
| P-06 Formulaic Transfer Hedge | No | No transfer claim made across populations. |
| P-07 Trust Without Process | **Yes** | The finding states a conclusion ("will increase engagement") with no reasoning connecting the evidence to the conclusion. There is no stated argument for *why* "clearer icon" implies "more engagement" — the inference is just asserted. A reader cannot reconstruct the reasoning because none is shown. |
| P-08 Narrowing Sold as Resolving | **Yes** | The evidence narrows the hypothesis space ("the new icon is at least noticeable") and this is being sold as resolving a different, larger question ("engagement will go up"). The causal verb "will increase" overclaims — the evidence supports at most "worth investigating whether the clarity gain translates to engagement." |

Six of eight pitfalls fire. This is a claim that should not be acted on in its current form.

### What would change this verdict
An A/B test (or equivalent behavioural measurement) comparing engagement metrics — specifically defined, e.g. tap-through rate on the feature — between the old and new icon, with enough users to detect a meaningful effect. That would move this from WEAK/unsupported to a verdict that could be STRONG or decisively negative. Without behavioural data, no amount of additional interview quotes about clarity will settle the engagement question, because they're measuring a different thing.

### Tacit-knowledge prompt
If the researcher who ran INT-01 is available: when the participant said "I spotted it right away," was that in response to a direct prompt about the icon (in which case demand characteristics are a real risk), or an unprompted comment during a task where they were looking for something else (which would be a much stronger visibility signal)? The distinction matters because it changes what the one data point is worth.

---

**Summary for the requester:** The claim as written is not a finding — it's a hypothesis dressed as a conclusion. The evidence supports "one user found the new icon easier to spot." Getting from there to "engagement will increase" requires either (a) a weaker, honest reformulation ("one user reported the new icon is easier to spot; worth testing whether this translates to engagement") or (b) actual engagement data. Recommend not acting on this claim until one of those two exists.
