# QA Readout — Onboarding Comprehension Study

## Finding: "Users think the price is too high."

**Evidence strength: WEAK**
A single off-topic remark from one of seven participants cannot support a plural claim about user price perception, and the finding isn't even addressing the question the study was designed to answer.

### Provenance

| Claim | Source | Observed or inferred | Traceable? |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Users" (plural) think the price is too high | INT-03 only (1 of 7 sessions) | Inferred — generalised from n=1 | Partially — one participant said something adjacent, but the plural generalisation is not traceable |
| The price is "too high" | INT-03: "$12 a month feels steep for what this does" | Inferred — "feels steep" is reframed as a definitive judgment ("too high") | No — the quote is a hedged impression, not a verdict |
| This reflects a view about pricing per se | INT-03 quote | Inferred — the phrase "for what this does" ties the complaint to perceived *value*, not price in isolation | No — the finding drops the conditional clause that changes its meaning |

The remaining six participants (INT-01, INT-02, INT-04, INT-05, INT-06, INT-07) are not cited at all. We have no evidence of what they said about price — including whether they said nothing, which would itself be relevant.

### Falsification pass
- **Strongest counter-hypothesis:** INT-03's remark is an off-hand aside in a study about onboarding comprehension — not pricing — and the other six participants did not raise price at all. A single unprompted comment in a study not designed to surface pricing attitudes is weak evidence that any shared user view exists. A competing read: INT-03 is signalling a *value* concern ("for what this does"), which may itself be a downstream symptom of the onboarding comprehension problem the study was actually built to investigate.
- **What would overturn this finding:** Evidence from the other six sessions showing price was raised unprompted and consistently, or a pricing-focused study (survey, pricing-sensitivity interviews, churn analysis tied to price) showing the pattern at scale. Alternatively, confirmation that INT-01, INT-02, INT-04–07 were asked about price and did not raise it — which would actively weaken the finding further.
- **Where the evidence underdetermines the claim:** Almost entirely. We have one hedged quote from one participant in a study not scoped to pricing. The evidence base cannot support a claim about "users" as a group, cannot distinguish price-concern from value-concern, and cannot tell us whether the other six participants agree, disagree, or were never asked.

### Pitfalls checked

| Pitfall | Triggered? | What triggered it |
|---|---|---|
| P-01 Headline Generality | **Yes** | "Users think the price is too high" is a label, not a behaviour. No trigger, no consequence, no observable action — just an attributed attitude. The sentence would read identically in a report about any subscription product. |
| P-02 Context Blindness | No (cannot assess) | No prior pricing research is referenced in the study record. If prior pricing work exists, the finding doesn't engage with it; if not, this pitfall can't be evaluated from what was provided. Flag for the researcher: is there prior pricing research this should be checked against? |
| P-03 Transcript Literalism | **Yes** | The quote "feels steep for what this does" is treated as a flat verdict on price. "Feels" is a hedge, and "for what this does" is a conditional that ties the complaint to perceived value. The written text loses the tone and context that would tell us whether INT-03 was venting, negotiating, or stating a firm view — and the synthesis makes no room for that ambiguity. |
| P-04 Volume Mistaken for Strength | **Yes (inverted form)** | The finding uses the plural "users" on the basis of n=1 of 7. This is the volume-vs-strength error at its most extreme: one participant generalised to a user population. Even if ten participants had said the same thing in the same study, they'd be one source type (moderated interviews in an onboarding study) — but here it isn't even one source type, it's one person. |
| P-05 Counter-Evidence Filed, Not Faced | **Yes (by omission)** | The six participants who did *not* raise price are counter-evidence to a claim about "users" generally, and the finding doesn't acknowledge their silence at all. Six out of seven sessions producing no pricing signal is itself a signal, and it's missing from the reasoning. |
| P-06 Formulaic Transfer Hedge | No | No transfer claim is being made across populations or markets. |
| P-07 Trust Without Process | **Yes** | The finding states a conclusion with no reasoning chain. There's no derivation shown — just the claim. A reader cannot reconstruct how one hedged quote became a plural attributed belief. |
| P-08 Narrowing Sold as Resolving | **Yes (adjacent form)** | The finding is scoped at the wrong level: it treats a single remark as resolving a question about user price perception that the study was not designed to answer. The study's actual question (Q-01, onboarding comprehension) is sidestepped, and a pricing conclusion is substituted without the evidence base to support it. |

Seven of eight pitfalls fire or partially fire. This is unusually bad; most findings trigger one or two.

### What would change this verdict
A dedicated pricing study — even a short one. A survey question on price perception administered to a reasonable sample, or a pricing-sensitivity interview round, would give an actual evidence base. Short of that, at minimum: a review of what the other six participants said (or didn't say) about price, and a rewrite of the finding to accurately reflect that one participant made a hedged value-related comment in a study about onboarding.

Separately: the finding should be removed from this study's output entirely. It isn't answering Q-01, and keeping it muddies the actual onboarding question the study was built to inform.

### Tacit-knowledge prompt
For the researcher who moderated INT-03: when the participant said "$12 a month feels steep for what this does" — was this raised unprompted, or in response to a question? And did any of INT-01, INT-02, or INT-04–07 touch on price, cost, or value during their sessions, even in passing? The answers would tell us whether we have one off-hand aside or the visible tip of a pattern the study wasn't built to capture.

---

**Note on scope:** This finding ("Users think the price is too high") is not addressing the study's stated question (Q-01: Do new users understand what the app does after onboarding?). Before acting on this QA at all, confirm with the researcher whether this claim was meant to be a finding of *this* study, or whether it belongs in a separate pricing investigation. If the former, the finding should be dropped or substantially rewritten; if the latter, it needs its own evidence base.
