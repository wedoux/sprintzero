## QA Readout

## Finding: "Reordering the settings menu into three sections instead of one long list is the right call."

**Evidence strength: WEAK**
There is no evidence supplied with this claim, no prior study referenced, and no behavioural signal described — the claim is an assertion of a design decision, not a research finding, and cannot be evaluated as research.

### Provenance

| Claim | Source | Observed or inferred | Traceable? |
|---|---|---|---|
| The settings menu is currently one long list | Not cited | Unstated — treated as given | No — no evidence provided that this is the current state |
| Reordering into three sections is "the right call" | Not cited | Inferred (asserted) | No — no evidence provided for why three sections, why not two or four, or why reordering is warranted at all |
| Users have a problem with the current menu | Not cited (not even stated) | Inferred by implication only | No — the claim implies a problem exists without naming it |

Every substantive component of this claim is unsupported. The belief register, study record, and evidence sections are all empty. There is nothing to trace.

### Falsification pass
- **Strongest counter-hypothesis:** Users do not have a navigation problem with the current settings menu, and reordering will introduce a transition cost (relearning, broken muscle memory, broken documentation and support macros) with no offsetting benefit. Equally plausible on the evidence provided — which is none.
- **What would overturn this finding:** Any evidence at all would move this discussion forward — e.g., task-completion data showing users fail to find specific settings, support ticket volume citing menu navigation, a tree-test or first-click study comparing the current structure against the proposed three-section structure, or interview quotes describing confusion. Also needed: a rationale for *three* sections specifically, with the proposed grouping named.
- **Where the evidence underdetermines the claim:** Everywhere. The claim is underdetermined in full. We don't know (1) that a problem exists, (2) that reordering addresses it, (3) that three sections is the right count, (4) what the three sections would contain, or (5) what alternatives were considered.

### Pitfalls checked

|---|---|---|
| P-01 Headline Generality | **Yes** | "The right call" is a verdict label with no observable behaviour, no trigger, and no stated consequence. The sentence would read identically for almost any product's settings menu — it fails the "any other product" test. |
| P-02 Context Blindness | **Yes** | Study record is empty. We don't know whether prior research exists on this menu, whether a previous reorganisation was tried, or whether this contradicts earlier findings. The claim is presented as if the slate is blank; it may not be. |
| P-03 Transcript Literalism | No | No transcript or source material is cited to be over-trusted. (The problem here is the opposite — nothing is cited at all.) |
| P-04 Volume Mistaken for Strength | No | No evidence volume is being claimed. (Not applicable because there is no evidence, not because the claim passes the test.) |
| P-05 Counter-Evidence Filed, Not Faced | No | No counter-evidence is named — so none is being filed-and-ignored. The absence is itself a problem, but it is not this pitfall. |
| P-06 Formulaic Transfer Hedge | No | No transfer claim is being made across populations. |
| P-07 Trust Without Process | **Yes** | The verdict "right call" is asserted without any reasoning chain. There is no derivation to audit — just the conclusion. A reader cannot reconstruct how this classification was reached because no reasoning was shown. |
| P-08 Narrowing Sold as Resolving | **Yes** (borderline) | "Reordering is the right call" is a causal claim — it asserts that this change *is correct*, not that reordering is a hypothesis worth investigating. Even if some evidence existed that the current menu is confusing, that would at best narrow the search space ("menu structure is a candidate problem area"), not resolve what to do about it. |

### What would change this verdict
A single piece of actual evidence that users have difficulty with the current settings menu — e.g., a tree-test result, support ticket data, task-completion metrics from a usability session, or interview excerpts describing confusion — combined with a stated rationale for why *three* sections (and which three) addresses that specific difficulty. Without any of this, the claim cannot be evaluated as a research finding; it is a design proposal awaiting justification.

### Tacit-knowledge prompt
Was this claim meant to be submitted as a research finding, or as a design decision the team has already made on other grounds (strategic, aesthetic, consistency with another product)? The QA pass evaluates research conclusions against evidence; if this is a design call being made for non-research reasons, the appropriate review is a design critique, not a research QA. Clarifying which it is would determine whether this skill is the right tool at all.

**Meta-note on this review:** This claim arrived with no belief register, no study record, and no evidence. The honest QA output in that situation is to say so plainly rather than manufacture a nuanced-looking review. There is no research here to pressure-test — only an unsupported assertion. The verdict is WEAK not because the proposed menu change is wrong (it may well be right), but because nothing has been offered that would let anyone tell.
