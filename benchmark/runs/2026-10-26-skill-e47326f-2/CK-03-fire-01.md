## Finding: "It's now proven that users prefer voice input over typing."

**Evidence strength: WEAK**
This claim overreaches badly — two interview quotes about speed preference cannot "prove" a general preference for voice over typing across users, contexts, or tasks.

### Provenance

| Claim | Source | Observed or inferred | Traceable? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Users prefer voice input over typing | INT-01, INT-02 quotes | Inferred | Partially — two participants expressed a speed-related preference; the general claim extends far beyond what they said |
| This is "proven" | No evidence | Inferred (unsupported) | No — nothing in the evidence set supports a proof-strength claim |
| Preference is for "voice input" specifically (vs. typing in general) | INT-01, INT-02 | Inferred | Weak — participants said "say it out loud" and "talking," which may or may not map to a product's voice input feature; context is missing |

Neither quote mentions a specific product, task, or situation. We don't know what they were typing, on what device, under what conditions, or whether this preference holds beyond the moment they were asked.

### Falsification pass
- **Strongest counter-hypothesis:** These two participants prefer talking over typing *in some contexts* (e.g., long-form input, mobile, hands-busy situations) but not others (e.g., precise edits, public settings, technical content). The quotes are compatible with a highly conditional preference, not a general one.
- **What would overturn this finding:** Evidence from the same or other participants showing they *choose* typing when voice is available — e.g., observed behaviour in a session, a quote like "but I'd never use voice at work," or survey data showing mixed preferences across tasks.
- **Where the evidence underdetermines the claim:** Almost everywhere. We have n=2, single source type (interviews), no task context, no situational context, no behavioural observation (only stated preference), and no information about whether these participants are representative of any defined population.

### Pitfalls checked

| Pitfall | Triggered? | What triggered it |
|---|---|---|
| P-01 Headline Generality | **Yes** | "Users prefer voice input over typing" is a label with no trigger, no context, and no consequence. Could appear in a report about any product. Fix: specify *which* users, in *what* situations, for *what* tasks, and what observable behaviour would follow. |
| P-02 Context Blindness | Unknown | No prior research declared in the study record, so can't assess. Flagging that voice-vs-typing preference is a well-studied area and any real synthesis should check prior work before claiming novelty — let alone "proof." |
| P-03 Transcript Literalism | **Yes** | Treating two short quotes as a settled preference ignores everything the transcript doesn't capture: were they frustrated in the moment? Comparing voice to a specific bad typing experience? Would they say the same tomorrow? No mechanism in the synthesis for surfacing this. |
| P-04 Volume Mistaken for Strength | **Yes** | Two quotes from the same source type (interviews) saying similar things is one signal repeated, not corroboration from independent angles. No reviews, no analytics, no observed behaviour. STRONG/"proven" language is unsupported by source diversity. |
| P-05 Counter-Evidence Filed, Not Faced | No | No counter-evidence is named in the synthesis — but this is itself a problem (the synthesis didn't look for any), not a pass. |
| P-06 Formulaic Transfer Hedge | No | No transfer claim is being made (no geography/population extension stated). |
| P-07 Trust Without Process | **Yes** | The word "proven" is a conclusion asserted without any reasoning chain. There is no stated argument for *why* two quotes constitute proof, because no such argument is possible. The classification is the label, nothing more. |
| P-08 Narrowing Sold as Resolving | **Yes** | Even if the preference were real, "users prefer voice" would narrow the design space (consider voice input), not resolve the product question (will adding voice input improve outcomes?). The claim treats a weak signal as a solved problem. |

### What would change this verdict
The single most useful next piece of evidence: **observed behaviour** showing whether these (or similar) users actually choose voice over typing when both are available, across more than one task type. Stated preference in an interview is the weakest form of evidence for a behavioural claim; a brief diary study or usage analytics from a product with both input modes would move this from "two people said a thing" toward something a team could act on.

Secondary: a defined population. "Users" is doing enormous work in this claim. Two interviews cannot speak for a user base without a stated sampling frame.

### Tacit-knowledge prompt
If a researcher ran these interviews: what were INT-01 and INT-02 doing or being asked about when these quotes came up? Were they describing a specific recent frustration (e.g., typing on mobile, dictating a long message), or a general preference across all input situations? The answer determines whether this is a narrow context-specific preference or something broader — and the quotes alone can't tell us.

---

**Overall:** This is not a finding ready for a product decision. It is a hypothesis worth investigating, stated as a proven conclusion. The word "proven" should be struck entirely; "two interview participants expressed a speed-based preference for speaking over typing in unspecified contexts" is the honest version of what the evidence supports.
